\section{About ontologies}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{What is an ontology?}

  \begin{block}{}
    A knowledge classification of a domain, where the relationships
    between concepts are formally defined and logically related, which
    allows for computational reasoning.
  \end{block}

  \bigskip
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[nodes={shape=rectangle,draw,fill=GenericItem}]
      \node (imaginaldisc) {imaginal disc};
      \node [right=of imaginaldisc] (multicellstr) {multi-cellular structure};
      \node [right=of multicellstr] (organismsdiv) {organism subdivision};
      \node [below=of imaginaldisc] (wingdisc)     {wing disc};
      \node [below=of multicellstr] (wing)         {wing};
      \node [below=of organismsdiv] (thorax)       {thorax};
      \node [below=of wingdisc]     (wingpouch)    {wing pouch};
      \node [below=of wing]         (wingblade)    {wing blade};
      \path [IsARel]     (imaginaldisc) -- (multicellstr) node[midway,RelationLabel] {is\_a};
      \path [IsARel]     (wingdisc) -- (imaginaldisc)     node[midway,RelationLabel] {is\_a};
      \path [IsARel]     (wing) -- (multicellstr)         node[midway,RelationLabel] {is\_a};
      \path [IsARel]     (thorax) -- (organismsdiv)       node[midway,RelationLabel] {is\_a};
      \path [PartOfRel]  (wing) -- (thorax)               node[midway,RelationLabel] {part\_of};
      \path [PartOfRel]  (wingblade) -- (wing)            node[midway,RelationLabel] {part\_of};
      \path [PartOfRel]  (wingpouch) -- (wingdisc)        node[midway,RelationLabel] {part\_of};
      \path [DevFromRel] (wing) -- (wingdisc)             node[midway,RelationLabel] {develops\_from};
      \path [DevFromRel] (wingblade) -- (wingpouch)       node[midway,RelationLabel] {develops\_from};
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}

  \note{An ontology is basically a representation of a domain of knowledge, in
  such a way that it can be manipulated deterministically by a computer.
  
  It is a set of concepts, also variously known as ``classes'' or ``terms'',
  linked together by relationships that are themselves defined in the ontology.
  
  You have here an excerpt from the \emph{Drosophila Anatomy Ontology}, an
  ontology that aims to describe the anatomy of the fruit fly \emph{Drosophila
  melanogaster}, where the concept of a fly wing is described by relationships
  with several other concepts of fly anatomy: a subsumption relationship with
  the concept of muticellular structure (a wing \emph{is a} multicellular
  structure), a composition relationship with the concept of thorax (a wing
  \emph{is part of} the throax, or the thorax \emph{has} wings), and a
  developmental relationship with the concept of wing disc, which indicates that
  an adult fly's wing is formed from a particular part of the larva called the
  wing disc.
  
  In the end, an ontology allows to describe arbitrarily complex concepts by a
  combination of simple ``sentences'' or ``axioms'', of the form subject -- verb
  -- complement; or as they are called in the field, subject -- predicate --
  object.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]
  \frametitle{Controlled vocabularies, taxonomies, ontologies?}

  \begin{columns}[t]
    \begin{column}{.45\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Controlled vocabulary}
        A flat list of terms, without any relationship between them.

\begin{Verbatim}
multicellular structure
organism subdivision
thorax
wing blade
wing disc
wing pouch
\end{Verbatim}
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{.45\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Taxonomy}
        A controlled vocabulary where terms are connected through ``is
        a'' relationships.

\begin{Verbatim}
organism subdivision
  thorax
multicellular structure
  wing blade
  wing disc
  wing pouch
\end{Verbatim}
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  
  \begin{block}{Ontology}
    A taxonomy where the relations between the terms are not limited
    to ``is a'', but are instead terms themselves.
  \end{block}

  \note{A short detour to explain how ontologies are related to the
  similar concepts of ``controlled vocabularies'' and ``taxonomies''.

  One way to look at those concepts is to see them as a progression from
  controlled vocabularies to taxonomies to ontologies. In that view, a
  controlled vocabulary is merely a flat list of terms. For example
  here, terms related to the anatomy of the fruit fly \emph{Drosophila
  melanogaster}; there are no connections between the terms, and the
  terms are typically not really defined.

  A taxonomy is a controlled vocabulary in which the terms are organised
  in a hierarchy where a child term has a narrower meaning that its
  parent term. We say that the terms are connected by \emph{is-a}
  relations, for example a ``wing blade'' is a ``multicellular
  structure''.

  An ontology is the ultimate step of that evolution, and can be seen as
  a taxonony where terms can be related by \emph{arbitrary} relations,
  which are themselves defined in the ontology, instead of only
  \emph{is-a} relations.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Biomedical ontologies}

  \begin{block}{Gene Ontology (1998)}
    Describes the functions, processes, and subcellular locations
    associated with genes and gene products.
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Open Bio-Ontologies (OBO) Foundry (2002)}
    A community of ontology developers agreeing on a shared set of
    principles.

    \bigskip
    From 60 bio-ontologies in 2007 to 245 in 2021.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Gene Ontology (GO)
      \item Human Disease Ontology (DOID)
      \item \emph{Drosophila} Anatomy Ontology (FBbt)
      \item Zebrafish Phenotype Ontology (ZP)
      \item ...
      \item Biological Imaging Methods Ontology (FBbi)
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \note{Ontologies are nowadays used in almost all fields of scientific
  research, but here we'll focus specifically on ontologies used in
  biological and medical research.

  One of the first and most important biomedical ontologies is the Gene
  Ontology, created in 1998. At the time, progress in molecular biology
  meant that biologists were accumulating a lot of knowledge about
  genes: what a given gene does, which biological processes it is
  involved in, which parts of a cell its product is found in. This
  created a need to have a way to store that knowledge in databases, and
  this is what the Gene Ontology was designed to do.

  In the following years, several other ontologies were created on the
  model of the Gene Ontology, and this led to the formation of the Open
  Bio-Ontologies Foundry (OBO Foundry) in 2002, a group of ontology
  developers agreeing on a set of principles to follow in order to
  facilitate interoperability across ontologies.

  The OBO Foundry now regroups more than 200 ontologies covering various
  domains of biology and medicine. One of those ontologies is the
  ontology for biological imaging methods, or FBbi.}
\end{frame}


\section{About FBbi}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{A short history of FBbi}

  \begin{itemize}
    \item Initially created in the early 2000s at FlyBase to annotate
    the collection of fly images hosted at FlyBase
    \item Last release by FlyBase in 2005, when FlyBase stopped doing
    image curation
  \end{itemize}

  \bigskip
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Later successively maintained by
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Chris Woodcock for the Cell Image Library project;
      \item Willy Wong for the Center for Research in Biological Systems;
      \item David Osumi-Sutherland for the PhenoImageShare project.
    \end{itemize}
    \item All maintenance stopped at $\sim$2019.
  \end{itemize}

  \bigskip
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Maintenance resumed by German BioImaging for the FoundingGIDE
    project in early 2026.
  \end{itemize}

  \note{The FBbi ontology was created at the beginning of the century
  by curators at FlyBase.org, the database for \emph{Drosophila
  melanogaster} data. That's why the ontology is commonly known under
  the name ``FBbi'', which stands for ``FlyBase Biological Imaging''.
  FlyBase curators needed such an ontology to annotate the collection of
  fly images that was available in the database. They developed the
  ontology for about 5 years, until FlyBase stopped maintaining its
  image collection, which removed the need, at least as far as FlyBase
  was concerned, for an ontology of biological imaging methods.

  The need for such an ontology still existed outside of FlyBase though,
  and so over the years several groups took over the responsibility of
  maintaining the ontology and ensuring it was kept in sync with the
  progress of biological imaging. However all those efforts came to a
  complete end in 2019, leaving the ontology completely unmaintained.

  Since an up-to-date ontology for imaging method is \emph{still}
  needed, German BioImaging and the FoundingGIDE project took ownership
  of the ontology earlier this year.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Overview of FBbi}

  As of the January 2026 release:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item 649 terms
  \item Organised under 9 top-level categories
    \begin{itemize}
      \item contrast-enhancing method
      \item detection method
      \item illumination method
      \item imaged parameter
      \item imaging method
      \item resolution-enhancing method
      \item sample preparation method
      \item source of contrast
      \item visualization method
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}

  \note{Here is a very quick overview of the contents of the FBbi
  ontology, as it was at the time of the last release earlier this
  year.

  The ontology currently contains 649 terms, grouped into 9 high-level
  categories as shown here.

  Of note, this organisation is not ideal, and we will most likely
  overhaul it over the next few months. The terms themselves will still
  remain valid, though.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Example term: FBbi:00100016}

  \begin{block}{Label}
    ``cryogenic scanning electron microscopy''
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Synonyms}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item cryo-scanning electron microscopy
      \item cryo-SEM
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Definition}
    ``A form of scanning electron microscopy in which the sample is
    maintained in a vitrified state at cryogenic temperatures throughout
    imaging (PMID:40345995).''
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Relationships}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \texttt{is\_a} ``scanning electron microscopy'' (FBbi:00000257)
      \item \texttt{makes\_use\_of} ``cryo-fixed tissue'' (FBbi:00000013)
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}

  \note{Here is what a single term in the ontology look like.

  For any given term, you have:

  (1) a fixed ID, that you can use to reliably refer to the term;

  (2) a label, along with synonyms when appropriate, so that the term
  can be more easily found even if the same technique is known by
  several slightly different names;

  (3) a definition (at least in theory; in reality, most terms in FBbt
  currently don't have a definition; we're working on fixing that), so
  that when you are looking for a term, once you have found it you can
  check that it does indeed refer to the concept you were looking for;

  (4) relationships that connect the term to other terms in the
  ontology.}
\end{frame}


\section{Using FBbi}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Browsing the ontology locally}

  Ontology editor of choice: Protégé (desktop application available for
  GNU/Linux, macOS, and Windows)
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Download it from \url{https://protege.stanford.edu}
    \item Then download FBbi itself from \url{http://purl.obolibrary.org/obo/fbbi.owl}
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[scale=.16]{protege}
  \end{center}

  \note{
    If you want to use FBbi terms to annotate your images, you will need
    to find the terms that correspond to the methods that were used for
    any given image. In turn, this means you need to be able to browse
    and search the ontology.

    You can do that either locally or online.

    To do it locally, I recommend the Protégé ontology editor, which we
    use to \emph{edit} the ontology but that can be used as a viewer or
    browser (full disclosure: I am the maintainer of that editor as
    well). With Protégé installed on your machine, you can then download
    the latest version of the ontology, open it in Protégé, and then
    explore the contents of the ontology as much as you want.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Browsing the ontology online}

  Several ontology browsers are available online:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Ontobee: \url{https://ontobee.org/ontology/FBbi}
    \item OLS: \url{https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ols4/ontologies/fbbi}
    \item AberOWL: \url{http://aber-owl.net/ontology/FBBI/\#/}
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[scale=.14]{ols}
  \end{center}

  \note{If you only need a FBbi term only very occasionally, then you
  might prefer avoid having to download a specialised program just to
  browse an ontology. In that case, you can use some online ontology
  browsing services, such as those mentioned here.}
\end{frame}


\section{Contributing to FBbi}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{You need something? Ask!}

  \begin{block}{}
    The easiest way for anyone to contribute to FBbi is to open a ticket
    on \url{https://github.com/foundingGIDE/fbbi/issues}.
  \end{block}

  \bigskip
  Whether you
  \begin{itemize}
    \item need of a term that is missing from the ontology,
    \item think that the definition of an existing term is perfectible
    \begin{itemize}
      \item (Definitions in an ontology are \emph{always} perfectible!),
    \end{itemize}
    \item think that a term is mis-classified,
    \item or have any other problem with the ontology as it currently
    is,
  \end{itemize}

  \bigskip
  Let us know about your problem so that we can fix it!

  \bigskip
  We are already aware of some problems but an ontology is always
  dependent on its users!

  \note{Now if you are using FBbi but have a problem with it, what can
  you do? You can ask for your problem to be fixed. 

  If you are using an imaging method that is currently not represented
  in the ontology, if you don't like the way a method is defined, if you
  think a term is misplaced within the hierarchy, or more generally if
  there's anything preventing you doing what you need with FBbi...

  Reach out to us by opening a ticket on the ontology's issue tracker.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Want to contribute more directly?}

  \begin{block}{}
    Reach out first on the issue tracker to let us know about the
    contribution(s) you are planning to make.
  \end{block}

  What do you need to contribute?

  \begin{itemize}
    \item Software
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Protégé
      \item \href{https://github.com/INCATools/ontology-development-kit}{Ontology Development Kit} (*)
      \item Git (version control system)
    \end{itemize}
    \item Skills
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Domain expertise
      \item Familiarity with the Web Ontology Language (OWL) (*)
    \end{itemize}
    \item Documentation
    \begin{itemize}
      \item ``\href{https://oboacademy.github.io/obook/}{OBO Semantic Engineering Training}''
      \item Join the OBO community on Slack (\url{https://obo-communitygroup.slack.com})
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}

  (*) Depending on the complexity of the changes you want to make.

  \note{Should you want to contribute more directly, you can also edit
  the ontology yourself, and then propose your contributions to us.

  If you want to do that, you should still start by opening a ticket on
  the issue tracker, so that we know what you are planning to work on.

  In concrete terms, to edit the ontology you will mostly need the
  Protégé ontology editor. For some complex changes, you might have to
  also use a tool called the Ontology Development Kit, but that should
  not happen very often. You must also be somewhat familiar with the
  Git version control system, since all changes to the ontology are
  mediated through this system.

  In terms of skills, the most important skill is the expertise of the
  domain. You may also need to know a bit about the Web Ontology
  Language, which is the language in which the ontology is written, but
  for most changes this should not be needed.

  If you are interested and want to learn more, I invite you to check
  the training resources made available by the OBO Foundry, and to join
  the OBO Community on its Slack channel.}
\end{frame}
